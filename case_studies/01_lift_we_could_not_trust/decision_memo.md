# Decision memo: checkout widget redesign (experiment CHK-2026-03)

To: Checkout product lead
From: Experimentation analyst
Date: 24 March 2026, the interim look (the first 14 days of enrolment, their primary metric data complete; refund guardrail complete by design for these users on 6 April 2026). Simulated case: single-run figures come from seed 20260928, selected by a stated rule to show this pattern; rates come from 5,000 simulated replicates.

Recommendation: do not ship. Stop the experiment, repair the exposure instrumentation, validate it with an A/A run, and rerun the experiment under the same pre-declared design with the repair in place.

What the experiment was meant to decide: whether the redesigned widget raises the 7-day completed-order rate enough to justify its maintenance cost. The pre-declared rule was to ship if the lower bound on the relative intent-to-treat lift exceeds +1.0%, the payment-step and latency guardrails pass their margins, the refund guardrail shows no breach and no decision blocker is open.

What the dashboard showed on day 14: +6.4% relative lift, interval +1.6% to +11.5%, p = 0.009. Read at face value, that result would clear the ship rule.

What we found: the dashboard compares users with a logged render event, and those counts are not balanced. Assigned users split 139,836 treatment to 140,164 control (compatible with 50/50); users with a render event split 114,707 to 119,182 (chi-square 85.6). About 15% of users in both arms fall back to the legacy checkout and never render the widget, which is expected and harmless. The excess missing treatment users, about 4,200, are all on Android below version 8.4, where the redesigned widget's analytics emitter loses the render event for about 35% of the users it renders for. That segment converts at about half the rate of everyone else (1.74% against 3.34% in the control arm), so removing part of it from the treatment side alone inflates the treatment rate. The pre-treatment composition of the two exposed arms differs (6.7% against 10.1% on the affected segment); on the assigned population it does not.

How much that matters, measured on simulated replicates of this incident: the day-14 dashboard overstates the decision-relevant (intent-to-treat) lift by 2.20 points on average (1.75 of it is bias against the lift among rendered users, the rest the dilution of the ITT effect), its interval covers the ITT lift 85.8% of the time, and under a true null it would still report a significant positive lift 10.2% of the time, against 2.6% with no incident. The intent-to-treat estimate is unbiased with 94.7% coverage.

What the corrected analysis says: on the assigned population, the intent-to-treat lift is +1.9%, interval −2.2% to +6.1%, p = 0.36. On its own the interval would send the experiment on to its final look; with the exposure and latency blockers open, the rule gives no decision. Excluding Android below 8.4 from both arms gives a valid comparison for rendered users outside that segment (about 76% of eligible users): +3.8%, interval −0.7% to +8.6%, a lift on the triggered scale that the +1.0% floor does not apply to directly.

Guardrails: payment-step errors pass their +0.3-point margin (5.12% of treatment users against 5.16% of control users; upper bound +0.13 points). Latency is measured by the same lost event, so for the affected treatment users it is missing on 45% of loads against 15% elsewhere; the measured loads show no increase, but until the loss mechanism is verified the affected segment's latency cannot be trusted. Refunds can arrive up to 14 days after an order; on the orders whose window has closed the rates are 5.0% in both arms, and the lower bound of the interval (−20% to +29% relative) is below the +10% harm margin, so there is no breach so far. The refund guardrail is breach-only because at this volume a pass would need about seven times the orders, and it is complete for these users on 6 April.

Why the recommendation follows: the blockers stand regardless of the estimate. The exposure pipeline is broken on the treatment side, so any triggered analysis on the full population, including the one the dashboard runs, compares two populations that are no longer a random split; triggered analysis is sound when the trigger is logged the same way in both arms, and here it is logged differently. Shipping on the ITT number alone would ship a widget whose latency on older Android builds we cannot yet trust, before the refund guardrail has matured. A lift of the simulated size (+2.55% on all users) would clear the +1.0% bar only about one time in seven even on a clean rerun with both looks (on the primary metric alone), and confirming it with 80% probability would need about 2 million users per arm, roughly seven months of traffic, so the realistic question for the product team is whether confirming a lift of that size is worth it. The ITT data from this run stays valid but is not pooled with the rerun: a restart after the fix does not form the contiguous final look the boundary assumes, and the latency guardrail for these users cannot be recovered.

What would change the decision: exposure parity across arms after the emitter fix, confirmed by an A/A run; a lower ITT bound above +1.0% at the interim (95% interval) or final (97.5% interval) look; p95 latency within its +5% margin on the affected segment with a verified logging mechanism; no refund breach on the complete cohort.

Open risks: users on more than one device (not modelled); order arrival delay of up to 36 hours means any analysis before 24 March 12:00 UTC undercounts both arms equally; a novelty effect on the redesign is not distinguishable within a 7-day window.
