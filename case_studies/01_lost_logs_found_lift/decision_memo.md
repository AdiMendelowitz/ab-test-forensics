# Decision memo: assembly station redesign (experiment ASM-2026-03)

To: Production lead
From: Experimentation analyst
Date: 24 March 2026, the interim look (the first 14 days of intake, their primary metric data complete; return guardrail complete by design for these products on 6 April 2026). Simulated case: single-run figures come from seed 20260928, selected by a stated rule to show this pattern; rates come from 5,000 simulated replicates.

Recommendation: do not ship. Stop the experiment, repair the scan instrumentation, validate it with an A/A run, and rerun the experiment under the same pre-declared design with the repair in place.

What the experiment was meant to decide: whether the redesigned station raises the 7-day yield enough to justify its maintenance cost. The pre-declared rule was to ship if the lower bound on the relative intent-to-treat lift exceeds +1.0%, the packing-step and cycle-time guardrails pass their margins, the return guardrail shows no breach and no decision blocker is open.

What the dashboard showed on day 14: +6.4% relative lift, interval +1.6% to +11.5%, p = 0.009. Read at face value, that result would clear the ship rule.

What we found: the dashboard compares products with a logged scan, and those counts are not balanced. Routed products split 139,836 new station to 140,164 old station (compatible with 50/50); products with a scan split 114,707 to 119,182 (chi-square 85.6). About 15% of products in both arms take the legacy manual route and never reach the scanner, which is expected and harmless. The excess missing new-station products, about 4,200, are all from the older component batch (supply line A, batches 1 to 3), where, in the simulation, the redesigned station's scanner loses the scan for 35% of the products the new station handles. That batch yields at about half the rate of everything else (1.74% against 3.34% in the old-station arm), so removing part of it from the new-station side alone inflates the new-station rate. The pre-treatment composition of the two arms with a logged scan differs (6.7% against 10.1% from the older component batch); on the routed population it does not.

How much that matters, measured on simulated replicates of this incident: the day-14 dashboard overstates the decision-relevant (intent-to-treat) lift by 2.20 points of relative lift on average (SE 0.03; 1.75 of it is bias against the lift among products that reach the scanner, the rest the dilution of the ITT effect), its interval covers the ITT lift 85.8% of the time (SE 0.5), and under a true null it would still report a significant positive lift 10.2% of the time (SE 0.4), against 2.6% (SE 0.2) with no incident. The intent-to-treat estimate is unbiased with 94.7% coverage (SE 0.3); all rates are from B = 5,000 replicates.

What the corrected analysis says: on the routed population, the intent-to-treat relative lift is +1.9% (95% interval −2.2% to +6.1%, p = 0.36). On its own the interval would send the experiment on to its final look; with the scan and cycle-time blockers open, the rule gives no decision. Excluding the older component batch (supply line A, batches 1 to 3) from both arms gives a valid comparison for products that reach the scanner outside that batch (about 76% of eligible products): +3.8%, interval −0.7% to +8.6%, a lift on the triggered scale that the +1.0% floor does not apply to directly.

Guardrails: packing-step errors pass their +0.3-point margin (5.12% of new-station products against 5.16% of old-station products; upper bound +0.13 points). Cycle time is measured by the same lost scan, so for the affected new-station products it is missing on 45% of runs against 15% elsewhere; the measured runs show no increase, but until the loss mechanism is verified the older component batch's cycle time cannot be trusted. Returns can arrive up to 14 days after a finished product; on the finished products whose window has closed the rates are 5.0% in both arms, and the lower bound of the interval (−20% to +29% relative) is below the +10% harm margin, so there is no breach so far. The return guardrail is breach-only because at this volume a pass would need about seven times the finished products, and it is complete for these products on 6 April.

Why the recommendation follows: the blockers stand regardless of the estimate. In this simulation the scan pipeline loses scans on the new-station side, so any triggered analysis on the full population, including the one the dashboard runs, compares two populations that are no longer a random split; triggered analysis is sound when the trigger is logged the same way in both arms, and here it is logged differently. Shipping on the ITT number alone would ship a station whose cycle time on older component batches we cannot yet trust, before the return guardrail has matured. A lift of the simulated size (+2.55% on all products) would clear the +1.0% bar only about one time in seven (14%, analytic) even on a clean rerun with both looks (on the primary metric alone), and confirming it with 80% probability would need about 2 million products per arm, roughly seven months of throughput, so the realistic question for the production team is whether confirming a lift of that size is worth it. The ITT data from this run stays valid but is not pooled with the rerun: a restart after the fix does not form the contiguous final look the boundary assumes, and the cycle-time guardrail for these products cannot be recovered.

What would change the decision: scan parity across arms after the scanner fix, confirmed by an A/A run; a lower ITT bound above +1.0% at the interim (95% interval) or final (97.5% interval) look; p95 cycle time within its +5% margin on the older component batch with a verified logging mechanism; no return breach on the complete cohort.

Open risks: products tracked by more than one scanner (not modelled); records delay of up to 36 hours means any analysis before 24 March 12:00 UTC undercounts both arms equally; a novelty effect on the redesign is not distinguishable within a 7-day window.
